# Level and World Design

## Teach without text: introduce → develop → twist → conclude
1. **Introduce** a mechanic in a safe space where failure costs nothing.
2. **Develop** it with mild pressure (a pit under the jump, one enemy).
3. **Twist** it by combining with another mechanic or reversing expectations.
4. **Conclude** with a test that proves mastery, then reward.
This "kishōtenketsu" structure is how classic 8-bit games taught players with no tutorials.

## Pacing
Alternate tension and release. After a hard room, give a safe room with a reward or a view. Plot intensity over time — it should look like a rising sawtooth, not a flat line or a cliff.

## Readability
- Foreground (collidable) tiles must contrast strongly with background tiles. Use value (light/dark), not only hue.
- Hazards share a colour language across the whole game (e.g. magenta = harmful).
- Interactive objects animate or shimmer; scenery stays still.

## Screens and rooms
Screen-by-screen worlds (flip-screen) give each room one clear idea and a natural checkpoint. Keep entry points clear of hazards so the player is never hit on arrival. Every room should have a reason to exist: a mechanic, a secret, a reward or a breath.

## Landmarks and navigation
Give each region a distinct palette, music and silhouette landmark so players can build a mental map. Use gates (locked doors, unreachable ledges) to show the player what they will return to.

## Secrets
Reward players who test the edges: a cracked wall, an off-screen ledge, a pattern in the tiles. A good secret is noticeable in hindsight.

## Difficulty curve
Start below the player's skill, ramp gradually, and plateau briefly after spikes. Checkpoints should be close enough that a failure costs under ~60 seconds of replay.
